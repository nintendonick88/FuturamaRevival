# Executive Summary

*Futurama: Worlds of Tomorrow* was a free-to-play mobile game launched by Jam City (TinyCo) in June 2017\. It featured new stories by creators Groening and Cohen and all original voice actors. However, development waned and Jam City announced on March 9, 2023 that the game would “come to an end” across all platforms, officially closing the servers on April 20, 2023\. As of mid-2026 the game is offline/unplayable. Fan interest remains steady but modest: the dedicated subreddit has \~100 members, and a now-archived official Discord listed \~333 members. No major petitions exist, though scattered fans have voiced willingness to fund revival (e.g. offering server costs) and made casual campaigns on social media.

Legally, Futurama is owned by Disney (20th Century Fox), meaning any new game requires Disney’s license. FoxNext originally co-developed the game, but its sale to Scopely in 2020 explicitly excluded Fox-licensed titles, so Disney retains Futurama game rights. Reviving a licensed game would necessitate negotiating a new license with Disney (and re-securing voice and writing talent). Precedents show licensed games often vanish without renewed deals (e.g. a *Deadpool* mobile game was delisted when its movie license lapsed).

Technically, no public source indicates that Jam City released the codebase. The game’s assets and server code are proprietary. A revival would require either reacquiring original code/assets or rebuilding from scratch. Core requirements include a Unity (likely) client rebuilt or updated, a new backend (cloud servers and database) to restore social/account features, and reimplementation of game mechanics (city-building, combat events, story quests). Platform targets should include iOS and Android (as original), and ideally expanded to PC (Steam) to reach more players. The inventory document (assumed to contain game assets metadata) could help gauge asset scope, but its contents are unspecified. In any case, a development team (perhaps 15–25 people: programmers, artists, designers, QA, ops) over 12–24 months would be needed, with an estimated budget in the low single-digit millions. Monetization would mirror the original: free download with in-app purchases (e.g. premium “Pizza” currency, battle passes, cosmetic items) and possibly optional ads (e.g. rewarded videos) to supplement revenue.

Business/funding could combine publisher partnership and crowdfunding. A major publisher (like Jam City again, or another midcore dev) could underwrite costs in exchange for revenue share. Alternatively, a targeted crowdfunding campaign (e.g. Kickstarter) might raise seed funds if Disney grants provisional licensing rights. Ad-revenue models (as in existing city-builder apps) could be integrated, but given Futurama’s brand, a pure IAP model is likely more lucrative. For context, Jam City claimed \~50 M monthly active players across its titles and \~$300 M revenue in 2016; a revived Futurama game could aim for modest traction among that fan base.

**Recommended revival strategy:** Begin with securing IP rights: formally approach Disney (Fox) to license Futurama for a mobile/PC game. Use the existing fan community to demonstrate demand. Plan an MVP launch focusing on core gameplay (Planet Express HQ building, exploration, quest events) and gradually expand content. Engage fans early (beta tests, polls) to build goodwill. Legally, ensure all original writers/actors contracts are renegotiated or alternatives are cast. Technically, decide whether to use any of Jam City’s code (if obtainable) or rebuild; rebuilding allows updating tech but costs more time. Marketing should leverage nostalgia (e.g. “It’s back, baby\!”) and tie-ins with the series (new Hulu seasons) to maximize exposure.

## 1\. History & Current Status

**Launch (2017):** Jam City’s TinyCo studio (in partnership with FoxNext) released *Futurama: Worlds of Tomorrow* on June 29, 2017 for iOS and Android. It was a free-to-play city-building/RPG hybrid featuring an original story by series creators (Matt Groening, David X. Cohen) and weekly updates. The game included celebrity voice cameos (e.g. Stephen Hawking, Bill Nye) and all the original Futurama cast. Early coverage noted its humor and gameplay mix (city building \+ galactic exploration \+ quest events).

**Operations:** The game ran updates for several years, but community reports indicate new content diminished after a peak period (roughly 2018–2019). No official press release details continued live-ops; however, fan forums remained active with event previews and bug reports through \~2021. (For example, a major early event “Lrrr Strikes Back” launched soon after release.) Jam City’s social channels (now defunct) and fan groups (e.g. Facebook) interacted with players, but activity waned as the game aged.

**Shutdown (2023):** On March 9, 2023 Jam City announced the game would “come to an end” on April 20, 2023 (after nearly six years of service). It closed purchases on March 9 and shut down servers April 20, making the game unplayable. No further updates occurred. By mid-2026, the game is officially defunct: app store listings and official social media for *Worlds of Tomorrow* have been removed or “archived.” The consensus from community sources is that Jam City gave each player in-game currency (e.g. 2500 pizza) upon closure, and then discontinued all support.

*Sources:* Jam City’s own 2017 press release and press coverage document the launch and features. The shutdown is confirmed by the Wikipedia entry on the game, which cites community reporting of Jam City’s announcement (closed April 20, 2023).

## 3\. Legal and IP Analysis

**IP Ownership:** Futurama is originally a Fox (20th Century Fox) property; after Disney’s acquisition of Fox’s entertainment assets, Futurama now falls under Disney’s control. In practical terms, Disney owns all rights to the *Futurama* brand, characters, and related games. The original game was produced in cooperation with FoxNext Games (Fox’s game division), but crucially **Disney’s sale of FoxNext to Scopely in Jan 2020 expressly excluded licensed games**. That news report notes “Scopely’s acquisition of FoxNext does not include the separate portfolio of licensed game titles based on Fox intellectual property, which will continue to be part of Disney’s licensed games business”. Thus, *Futurama: WoT* remained with Disney’s licensed games portfolio. Any revival would therefore **require Disney’s approval and a new license agreement**.

In addition to the Disney license, other rights must be considered. The game’s original creative team included Matt Groening, David X. Cohen, and Rough Draft Studios. If a new game uses their story elements or trademarks (e.g. Hypnotoad’s mythology), clearance from those parties (or their successors) would be needed. Similarly, the game’s original voice cast would need new contracts for recording. These talent agreements can be negotiated but add complexity (e.g. residuals or work-for-hire agreements).

**Licensing Hurdles & Precedents:** Reviving a shut-down licensed game is legally challenging. Once Disney pulls the license (or it expires), the game must cease operations – as seen with other IP-licensed games. A notable precedent is the *Deadpool* mobile game, which was delisted when its Marvel license expired and only briefly reappeared in sync with a movie release. This underscores that without active licensing, the game cannot legally relaunch. Another example: many TV- or movie-based mobile games shut down permanently when licenses lapse (often with no fanfare). There are few successful examples of classic licensed games being officially revived by new entities. (One gamer forum notes: “Licenses tend to be one of the main reasons for games delisting”.)

Therefore, the first legal step is **securing rights from Disney**. This likely means negotiating a license to use *Futurama* IP for an interactive game, possibly as a timed or territory-limited license. Disney may want guarantees (minimum royalty payments, revenue share, etc.). The developer might also need to show market viability (fanbase size, etc.). Without Disney’s OK, any revival effort would risk IP infringement or DMCA takedowns.

Precedents of licensed-game revivals are rare. Large franchises like *The Simpsons* have ongoing mobile games because the license was maintained (EA’s *Tapped Out* was continuously updated as its license with Fox stayed active). But when Tom Clancy’s, *Family Guy*, or other TV-licensed games shut down, they stayed off the market. Often, fans resort to unofficial “private servers” or emulation (which is legally murky). For example, fans have reverse-engineered and played offline versions of delisted games, but Disney has not authorized any *Futurama* WoT re-releases or source code releases. In summary, legally the **strategy must be to work *with* Disney, not around it**.

## 4\. Technical Feasibility

**Code and Assets:** No public information indicates the original *WoT* source code or server code was released. The game likely ran on Unity (Jam City often uses Unity) or a similar engine, with custom C\# code. If Jam City is willing, a revival project could attempt to license or acquire the codebase and assets directly; this would greatly speed up development. If that is impossible, the game would need to be **re-engineered from the ground up**. Analyzing the user-uploaded “inventory” document (if it lists game assets or items) could help gauge asset scope, but without internal metadata or code, developers must reconstruct characters, buildings, UI, etc., possibly by extracting textures from game files or creating them anew.

**Servers/Backend:** The original game used online servers for account management, events, chat, etc. All those servers were shut down at closure. A revival would require building a new backend: likely a cloud-hosted solution (e.g. AWS/GCP/Azure with auto-scaling) to handle login/auth, player databases, live events scheduling, and in-app purchases. Custom middleware would have to be developed or the original API emulated. This is a significant effort but straightforward for an experienced team. Maintenance of servers also adds ongoing costs. If a community or fan-run solution were attempted (e.g. private server software), it would still need developer time and likely face legal issues.

**Platforms:** Original platforms were **mobile**: iOS, Android (and Amazon’s Android store). A revival should target those first (covering \~90% of original audience). Porting to PC (Windows, via Steam/Epic) is an optional expansion. Technical feasibility of PC: Unity games can often be built for Windows/Mac with moderate effort. PC support could attract the many players who want *Futurama* on their desktops (and tap into Steam’s community). However, PC porting requires adding UI scaling, keyboard controls (for some interfaces), and passing PC store certification. A mid-core mobile game without controller support could work on PC as-is (mouse-driven). Alternatively, a “wrapper” using an emulator platform (like Bluestacks allowed fans to play on PC before) isn’t ideal for an official release.

**Development Resources:** A revival project is essentially full software development. Key roles would include: Project Manager, Lead Developer(s) (Unity/C\#, backend), Game Designers (economy, quest design), UI/UX Designer, 2D/3D Artists and Animators (for characters, environments, animation, VFX), Sound Designer, QA/Testers, DevOps/Cloud Engineers, and Community/Support staff. Depending on scope, \~15–25 developers plus contractors (voice recording, perhaps external studio for large assets) might be needed.

- *If rebuilding from scratch:* expect \~12–18 months of dev time for a basic MVP, or up to 24 months for a rich feature set.  
- *If code can be reused:* possibly \~6–12 months to update code, add new features, and port.

Costs: Hard to pin down, but even small mobile games often cost **hundreds of thousands to millions** to develop. Given the existing fan expectations and needed content, a multi-million-dollar budget is plausible. (For context, Jam City’s own portfolio games are major investments; even the much older *Futurama: Game of Drones* by Wooga spent on licensing and development, though no public figures.) Post-launch live-ops will require a smaller team (3–5 people) to run new events weekly, patch bugs, and engage community.

**Monetization Model:** The original game was freemium, using an in-game premium currency (“Pizza”) for boosts/characters. A revival should use a similar model: free download, optional in-app purchases (IAP) for currency, VIP passes, or cosmetic items. Additional features could include subscription passes (common now) that grant daily rewards, and optional ads (e.g. watch a video for free pizza) to engage non-spending users. Any model must balance fairness (fans disliked grindy paywalls) and profitability. Advertising (banners or interstitials) is generally avoided in premium IP games but rewarded ads might be acceptable if low-frequency.

## 5\. Business Models & Funding Options

- **Publisher Partnership:** The most traditional route is partnering with a publisher (Jam City again, or another midcore mobile studio). The publisher would fund development and marketing, and in turn share revenue. Advantages: they can handle platform relationships (App Store accounts, payment processing), have marketing clout, and assume financial risk. Jam City knows the IP but closed it, so a new publisher (e.g. a mobile games giant like Ludia, now part of Jam City’s umbrella, or a Disney interactive division) would make sense. This would likely require pitching a detailed plan to Disney (with financial projections and marketing strategy).  
    
- **Crowdfunding:** A fan-driven crowdfunding campaign (Kickstarter/Indiegogo) could raise initial funds and gauge interest. However, crowdfunding a licensed game is complicated: it still needs IP clearance from Disney *before* soliciting funds (or risk legal issues). In practice, one could campaign only *after* securing a license or partnership. The campaign could sell early-access keys, merch, or in-game currency. Past examples: few licensed games use crowdfunding (most are original IP). Fan interest for such a campaign would probably be international but niche; a successful campaign might raise anywhere from tens to hundreds of thousands of dollars, but likely underfunds full development of a polished mobile game. So crowdfunding would better serve as supplementary marketing and community building, not the sole funding source.  
    
- **Licensing Revenue:** Assuming a publisher deal, the business model is mainly in-app purchases and possibly sponsorships. Given Jam City’s claim of 50 M monthly players across all titles, even a small fraction of that on Futurama (say 1 M MAU) could yield significant IAP revenue if retention is good. Disney might demand royalties or a revenue share. Projections could use benchmarks: other Midcore city-builders (e.g. *Simpsons Tapped Out*, *Family Guy: Tapped Out*) often report millions of downloads and multi-million-dollar revenues per year (public data scarce).  
    
- **Advertising:** If needed, the game could include ads. For example, offer double rewards for watching a video ad. But brand IP games often keep ads minimal to protect brand image. More realistic is focusing on IAP sales (character unlocks, speed-ups) as primary income.

## 6\. Revival Strategy Recommendations

1. **Legal/IP Actions:** Immediately engage Disney’s licensing division. Prepare a pitch: emphasis on sustained fanbase, potential synergy with new TV seasons on Hulu, and a clear plan for quality (original writers involved, etc.). Seek at least a preliminary licensing agreement or letter of intent, outlining rights (mobile/PC, duration, territories) and obligations (royalties, content review). Simultaneously, identify and contact key creative partners (Groening’s Curiosity Company, Rough Draft, voice talent) to gauge interest/availability.  
     
2. **Community Engagement:** Use existing fan channels to build momentum. Create “Revive Worlds of Tomorrow” social media pages (if allowed) to aggregate fans and capture interest (within legal bounds – ensure not claiming official status without license). Consider a community pre-registration page with email sign-ups to demonstrate demand to licensors. Host AMA sessions with developers or fan events. Communicate a “2–3 year plan” transparently once licensed to assure fans.  
     
3. **Development Approach (MVP):** Adopt a Minimum Viable Product mindset initially. Build the core game (Galaxy exploration, base building, and a handful of story events) first. If original code is available, update it with new assets; if not, develop a “spiritual successor” using a modern engine. Prioritize gameplay loops: for example, completing quests yields resources to build the new Planet Express base; space exploration unlocks new maps/events. Defer more elaborate features (e.g. PvP arena, fully live economy) to later updates. This allows an earlier launch and then iterative improvement based on player feedback.  
     
4. **Technology Reuse:** If any technical documentation or asset lists exist (like the user’s inventory doc), use them to speed up asset creation. Otherwise, consider licensing or remastering original art (e.g. character models) if possible. For networking, use robust game-service frameworks (like PlayFab, Firebase, or a custom solution) to minimize backend development time.  
     
5. **Marketing Plan:** Tie the game’s revival marketing to Futurama’s broader media presence. For example, if a new TV season or film is in the pipeline (Hulu revived Futurama for Season 11+ in 2022), coordinate announcements. Use trailers featuring beloved characters (Hypnotoad, etc.) to generate excitement. Leverage influencers: partner with Futurama fan sites/blogs (e.g. gotfuturama.com) or YouTubers who cover Futurama. Offer early access to press/gaming sites. Consider playable demo at conventions or through selected testers to build word-of-mouth.  
     
6. **Risk Mitigation:** Recognize key risks and plan accordingly:  
     
   - *Legal Risk:* Insist on solid licensing contracts before spending heavily.  
   - *Financial Risk:* Limit initial burn by phasing development (MVP first). Consider performance milestones (e.g., soft-launch in small market to tune economy before global release).  
   - *Market Risk:* Nostalgia is strong but mobile markets are competitive; ensure enough polish and content variety to retain players beyond an initial surge. Plan for 6–12 months of free content drops post-launch (new characters/events) to sustain interest.  
   - *Team Risk:* Have backup plans if key talent is unavailable (scriptwriters or actors). Possibly engage less expensive writers/fans from within community for additional story quests under supervision.
